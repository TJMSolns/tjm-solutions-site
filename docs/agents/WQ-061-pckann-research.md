## Deliverable 3: plain-English explanation

The chromatograph of a fuel, a chemical sample, or a piece of wood produces thousands of data points,
a rich fingerprint that is mostly noise for the purpose of telling one sample from another. This work
used a genetic algorithm, a search method modeled on natural selection, to evolve which handful of
those data points actually mattered: it generated many candidate subsets of features, tested how well
each subset separated known sample classes, and bred the better-performing subsets against each other
across generations until a small, effective feature set emerged. That is feature selection: finding
the signal inside a mostly-irrelevant dataset without a human having to guess which parts to look at.

Once the genetic algorithm had narrowed thousands of data points down to a manageable few, those
features were fed into a classification method combining principal component analysis (a way of
projecting high-dimensional data into a small number of the most informative dimensions) with a
nearest-neighbor classifier (a new sample is identified by comparing it to its closest known
neighbors in that reduced space) -- the PCKaNN approach named in the brief. Applied to fuels, this let
the team tell apart JP-4, Jet-A, and other aviation and diesel fuels from their chromatographic
fingerprints alone, useful for authenticating a fuel sample or tracing a spill back to its source.
The same combination of technique (evolve the relevant features, then classify by proximity in a
reduced space) generalized to other materials in this body of work, including distinguishing wood
species by Raman spectroscopy, which is the point worth carrying into any current framing: this was not
a one-off fuels trick, it was a general method for finding structure in noisy chemical data, applied
across several problems, twenty-five years before "feature selection" and "classification" became
everyday machine-learning vocabulary.
